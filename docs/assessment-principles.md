# Assessment principles and evidence map

Status: organizing principles agreed in conversation; questions and evaluation methods below are proposals. Updated October 21, 2026.

## Purpose, legibility, and readability organize the assessment

The user confirmed these as the core principles. Mood and harmony, and visual hierarchy, support the design’s purpose. Audience and reading conditions inform every area.

This map takes precedence over treating mood collection as the whole assessment. The existing six mood words and comparison protocol remain drafts within one part of this framework.

## Questions, evidence, and methods

| Area | Questions for the project brief | Evidence to collect | Proposed evaluation method | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | What must someone understand or do? Where will the design appear? Which text roles are needed? | Project goal, actual content, medium, required roles, source-stated intended uses | Check each proposed family and role against the brief; ask the reader to complete the intended task | A reasoned suitability explanation and unresolved requirements |
| Legibility | Which languages, symbols, and similar characters matter? At what sizes and under what viewing conditions? | Required characters, actual font-file mappings, rendered instances, observed character-identification errors | Check character availability first; then use brief identification/transcription tasks with confusable characters at intended settings | Context-specific observations; missing characters or confusion flagged |
| Readability | Will people scan labels or read passages? How long is the content? What conditions or reading needs matter? | Actual passages/layout, size, line spacing, line length, reading-task outcomes, comfort reports | Short reading or information-finding tasks; record accuracy, comprehension where relevant, time, and reported comfort separately | Evidence about reading in that setting, not a universal font grade |
| Mood and harmony | What character should the design convey? What images, palette, and visual style surround the text? | Agreed mood words, attribute-specific comparisons, preference responses, contextual mockups | Compare identical samples for one named attribute; separately evaluate fit with the surrounding design | Perceived-character evidence, preference, and contextual fit kept distinct |
| Hierarchy | What should someone notice first? Which headings, paragraphs, labels, and actions need distinction? | Role assignments, complete combinations, layout, size and weight relationships | Ask readers to find a heading, key fact, or action; inspect reading order and role recognition | Evidence that the typography communicates structure |

These methods are a project research proposal. No participants have completed them. Time or a preferred appearance alone does not establish better reading.

## Audience across the map

Ask about language, reading experience, accessibility needs, device, viewing distance, and situation. Do not infer ability or preferences from age or cultural identity alone. Use audience context to select tasks and interpretation, not to assign stereotyped font traits.

## Two activities with different participants

**Build the research records:** collect source facts and documented intended uses; review controlled font instances; collect judgments or task observations with clear provenance.

**Help someone choose fonts:** gather the project brief and preferences, filter practical requirements, show explained candidates, and provide optional trials. A future product need not require every visitor to perform a reading study. Research findings must travel with their applicable conditions and limits.

Do not treat a user’s desired mood as evidence that a font expresses that mood.

## Suggested assessment order

1. Project purpose, medium, actual information, audience needs, and text roles.
2. Practical requirements: language/characters, necessary styles, and 1/2/3-family choice.
3. Desired character, existing design, and optional visual preference comparisons.
4. Candidate suggestions with roles, reasons, evidence gaps, and starting settings.
5. In-context trials for legibility, readability, harmony, and hierarchy.
6. Adjust weights, sizes, spacing, and colors; record decisions in a typography style guide.

This preserves later experimentation while recognizing that text settings affect what is being evaluated. A modular scale supplies size candidates in step 6; it does not prove legibility, readability, or design fit.

## Requirements versus preferences

Known unmet required characters or necessary styles should not be compensated for by a high mood match. Unknown required evidence remains unknown and needs investigation or an explicit qualification.

Some properties can be checked before ranking. Others, such as comfort and hierarchy, need actual context and may remain untested when a shortlist is shown. A provisional suggestion must not be described as reader-validated.

Do not average all five areas into one quality percentage. Keep evidence and findings visible by area. No numeric weights or final ranking formula are approved.

## First research brief

Use the existing five-family batch and one clearly labeled example project: a small informational website where readers find an event detail and read a short explanation. This is a proposed test fixture, not a new product requirement.

Prepare a neutral heading, a short paragraph, an action label, and a required-character sample. Keep the same words and viewing conditions across candidates within a trial. Record exact variants and settings. Judge headings and paragraphs in separate conditions.

Define mood words before running mood trials, as requested, but do not make completing the mood vocabulary a prerequisite for unrelated source checks. Preserve “not tested” for reading and hierarchy until tasks are actually observed.
