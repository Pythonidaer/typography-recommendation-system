# Mood vocabulary for discussion

Current scope: this six-word proposal is historical preparation, not the catalogue vocabulary. The [broader attribute inventory](attribute-inventory.md) is the active discovery plan. Johnny asked us to investigate omitted design intentions before approving a fixed set.


Scope: this is one part of the framework in assessment-principles.md. Purpose, legibility, and readability remain the core principles; mood evidence does not replace reading, character, hierarchy, or contextual-fit checks.
Status: proposal, version 0.1. No mood definitions, source mappings, rating scale, or scores are approved yet. Establish understandable words before implementing a test.

## What we mean by mood

The impression a reader associates with a specific text sample. This is not the reader’s personality or a permanent psychological property of a whole font family. Use perceived character as the broader research term; mood is the simpler product label to test.

## Proposed starter vocabulary

These 6 are a manageable discussion set, not an exhaustive taxonomy. They were chosen to connect to available provider tags while leaving room for human review.

| Word | Working meaning | Boundary to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calm | Quiet and restful. | A calm impression does not necessarily mean thin, small, or easy to read. |
| Playful | Lighthearted and mischievous. | Playful does not automatically mean appropriate for children. |
| Cheerful | Upbeat and optimistic. | Distinguish a positive feeling from playfulness. |
| Energetic | Lively and active. | Distinguish energy from bold weight or merely attracting attention. |
| Elegant | Graceful and refined. | Distinguish refinement from ornate decoration or assumed luxury. |
| Rugged | Rough and sturdy. | This is an appearance impression, not a judgment of technical quality. |

People may interpret these differently. Review the definitions and then actual examples. A trait can be present together with another trait: calm and elegant can coexist. Do not force these words into opposite pairs.

## Keep other useful dimensions

Vintage and futuristic describe style associations. Business describes context. Loud describes attention or presence. They may help a recommendation, but they should not all be described as emotions. Preserve them as separate preference layers.

Warm/friendly, serious, romantic, mysterious, rebellious, and other words can join a candidate backlog when users need them. They are not rejected for lacking a convenient source tag. Adding one requires a useful definition, examples, and an evidence plan.

## Mapping to source data

Calm, Playful, and Rugged have exact provider label counterparts. Cheerful is a proposed interpretation of Happy; Energetic relates to Active/Excited; Elegant relates to Sophisticated/Fancy. Related words are not interchangeable numeric scales. None of these mappings is approved for scoring yet. Preserve source values separately.

Definitions here are project proposals, not Google definitions or conclusions established by research. Current five-family records contain uneven evidence for them, so they cannot supply a balanced set of validated high/low anchors.

## Establish a word before measuring fonts

1. Check whether Johnny understands the word and its definition as intended.
2. Ask whether it matters for a design and whether a different word would be clearer.
3. Identify overlapping words; keep them separate only if people can distinguish them usefully.
4. Prepare actual text examples with explicit settings and ask what fits the word.
5. Retain disagreement and revise unclear definitions before introducing numeric scores.

No claims about reader feelings, cultures, ages, or font suitability follow automatically from a mood label.

## Sources and rationale

Google Fonts documents a human tagging process based on comparison within a tag. That supports using existing labels as research inputs, not treating them as a universal mood taxonomy:
https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/tags.html

O’Donovan and colleagues (2014) collect attribute-specific font comparisons and estimate relative attributes. They distinguish individual font styles and report differences in agreement among attributes. This supports testing perceived character through comparisons. It does not validate our 6 words, 4-option format, or a new score formula:
https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~donovan/font/fontSelection.pdf

Jordan and colleagues (2017) study perceived print personality using Arabic typefaces. Their work motivates attention to script and study population; it does not establish that our English labels or source weights transfer unchanged:
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01229/full

These research summaries are intentionally limited. Protocol recommendations below are our proposals to test. See mood-review-protocol.md.
