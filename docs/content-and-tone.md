# Content and tone brief

Status: initial profile based on Johnny’s stated goals, ready to test in the font preview. Updated October 21, 2026. Recommendations and examples remain subject to review.

## Audience and task

Help people choose font families for a design, understand the reasons, and compare alternatives without needing specialist vocabulary. The current preview tests short explanations against a portfolio brief. It is not a validated automatic recommendation engine.

## Voice

Knowledgeable, approachable, concise, and lightly playful. Be a useful guide who can explain a recommendation in plain language. Establish authority through sound reasons, checked sources, and clear limits. Do not present the project as Google’s official recommendation service or claim established expertise or accuracy that has not been demonstrated.

Voice is the stable character of the product. Tone changes with the task. Writing style covers wording, structure, and mechanics.

## Tone profile

| Dimension | Project choice | How it appears in writing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formality | Conversational | Direct sentences, familiar words, natural contractions |
| Humor | Light and optional | A little personality in exploration; factual recommendation reasons |
| Respectfulness | Consistently respectful | Treat people as capable; avoid condescension and assumptions about their feelings |
| Enthusiasm | Interested and measured | Encourage comparison without hype, pressure, or a perfect-match claim |

These are qualitative positions, not numerical scores or a universal tone formula. The four dimensions follow the toolkit’s NN/g reference; the choices above are our project application.

## Adjust by context

| Context | Delivery |
| --- | --- |
| Character review | Minimal and neutral. Characters first, without mood descriptions or suggested roles beside the specimens. |
| Recommendation explanation | Specific and informative. Connect observed or documented features to the brief, then identify a useful tradeoff. |
| Exploration | Friendly, inviting, and occasionally playful. Preserve the option to reject every sample. |
| Instructions | Literal and brief. Labels say what an action does. |
| Missing evidence | State the uncertainty and the next useful check. Keep important limits near the decision. |
| Loading or failure | Calm and factual. Say what is happening or what failed and offer a supported recovery action. No jokes. |

Do not make every sentence playful. Clarity takes priority when personality competes with meaning. The font’s perceived mood and the product’s writing tone are separate decisions.

## Recommendation explanation pattern

After the entire character review, provide a short explanation for each family:

1. Suggested role, clearly described as a proposal.
2. Specific visible or documented characteristics relevant to the request.
3. The connection to the person’s brief and the family’s contribution to the combination.
4. A meaningful tradeoff or unresolved point.
5. A direct primary-source link for attributed design information.

Start with about 40-70 words per family as an editing target, not a hard limit. Link to deeper detail instead of explaining every possible attribute. Keep necessary qualifications even when they make a sentence longer. Avoid a repeated general disclaimer in every explanation; state shared limits once and retain family-specific limits where useful.

Facts about construction, designer intent, our recommendation reasoning, a person’s preference, and measured reading performance are different evidence types. Do not present a documented intended use as a verified reading result. Do not treat style-category membership as proof of suitability. No invented scores, match percentages, universal mood labels, or validated-pairing claims.

## Writing rules

Use plain English, active voice, sentence case, US spelling, and natural contractions. Replace avoidable jargon. If a specialist term helps someone decide, explain it briefly where it appears. For example, describe the small feet on letters before relying on “serif” with a beginner.

Keep headings literal and links specific. Remove obvious character-group labels, repeated instructions, slogans, filler, and promotional claims. No emoji, forced humor, fabricated urgency, exaggerated praise, or “Not X, but Y” framing. Preserve full source URLs in research documentation and specific linked names in the interface.

Choose font families before discussing color, weight, sizes, texture, motion, or other styling. The preview uses consistent reference settings to make letterforms visible; those settings are not a proposed final design.

## Examples

Useful explanation:

> Its squared letters and tapered corners echo the angular lettering in your reference. We’d try it for the opening headings. Its orderly shapes may feel too tidy for the darker section.

The visible features are supported by Chakra Petch’s source description; the connection to a project is our interpretation. A full explanation links its source.

Avoid:

> The ultimate retro powerhouse will instantly elevate your portfolio with unparalleled personality.

This makes no inspectable connection to the brief and overstates the result.

Exploration prompt:

> No favorite yet? We can explore other families.

Missing information:

> We haven’t tested this combination with your content yet.

## What to test

Can a reader explain why a family was suggested, identify the tradeoff, distinguish a documented feature from our interpretation, and choose what to compare next? Ask whether the wording feels useful and approachable. Do not assume that liking the tone establishes comprehension or recommendation accuracy.
