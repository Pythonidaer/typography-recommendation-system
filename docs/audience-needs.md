# Audience needs for typography recommendations

Status: source-informed planning proposal. Updated October 21, 2026. The dimensions and questions below are our synthesis, not a validated audience taxonomy or font scoring model.

## Start with needs and tasks

Keep the audience profile separate from the font record. A reader’s age, diagnosis, profession, or culture is not a permanent attribute of a typeface. Record the actual reading task, language, relevant access needs, context, and evidence. A person may have several overlapping needs and may encounter different conditions in different sessions.

Age can provide context, particularly when investigating reading development or age-related changes. It does not independently establish reading proficiency, vision, preferred appearance, technical familiarity, or the best font. Do not turn “children,” “seniors,” or a diagnostic category into an automatic font assignment.

The person taking the assessment is often a designer describing an audience, not a participant describing their own needs. Allow unknown answers, record where an assumption came from, and test with actual or likely readers before claiming the recommendation meets their needs.

## Sources reviewed

### GOV.UK: Learning about users and their needs

https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/start-by-learning-user-needs

The guide supports research into users’ goals, behaviour, problems, and circumstances, and describes profiles or personas grouping users with similar behaviour and needs. It recommends treating unsupported suggestions as assumptions and validating needs through research.

Application: organise profiles around what people need to accomplish, with evidence for each claim. This is general service-design guidance, not a predefined list of typography audiences or font ratings.

### W3C WAI: Diverse Abilities and Barriers

https://www.w3.org/WAI/people-use-web/abilities-barriers/

This educational resource groups barriers around auditory, cognitive and learning, physical, speech, and visual abilities. It emphasises individual variation, overlapping disabilities, skills, tools, and context. Its examples are not exhaustive.

Application: visual and reading needs are especially relevant to typography. Other needs can affect navigation and interaction, but should not be converted to a typeface preference without a reason. Do not assume all members of a group share the same needs.

### W3C: Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities

https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/

The document provides user needs, personas, objectives, and design patterns covering understanding, attention, memory, language, and task completion. It is a Working Group Note with supplemental guidance, not a mandatory WCAG requirement or evidence that a particular font is accessible.

Application: use its user stories to identify content and reading problems worth investigating. Include readers in research and testing. Do not diagnose people or assign a “cognitive accessibility” font score based on a family name.

### Microsoft Inclusive Design: Inclusive 101

https://inclusive.microsoft.design/articles/inclusive-101-guidebook

Its Persona Spectrum considers permanent, temporary, and situational mismatches and motivations.

Application: consider the reading environment alongside ongoing needs. A design may need to work when someone is distracted or under difficult viewing conditions. This framework does not rank fonts or replace user research.

## Proposed profile dimensions

| Dimension | Examples to investigate | What it informs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reader’s goal | Explore work, find an answer, learn a process, compare information, complete an action | Purpose and priority of text roles |
| Reading task | Glance at a heading, scan labels, read a passage, identify exact characters or numbers | Task-specific comparison and evaluation |
| Reading experience | Learning to read, fluent in the script, unfamiliar with the language, unfamiliar with the subject | Research questions and test content; not automatic font assignments |
| Language and script | Which text must be read in each language, and which exact characters are needed | Coverage requirements by role |
| Functional access needs | Distinguishing similar characters, following lines, attention support, magnification or text-to-speech use | Features to examine and conditions to test |
| Context and medium | Phone, desktop, printed material, signage, interrupted use, distance | Relevant evaluation conditions |
| Audience evidence | Observed, reported, documented, assumed, unknown | Confidence in the brief, not a font match score |

These dimensions overlap. They are starting fields for investigation, not a compulsory questionnaire or an exhaustive segmentation scheme. A screen reader need requires semantic text and compatible behaviour; a visible font choice alone cannot satisfy it. Knowing an audience’s subject expertise can change wording and explanations without changing the family selection.

## Connect to the three core principles

| Principle | Operational question | Proposed evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Does the treatment help people understand or do the intended thing? | Tasks, role assignments, finding information, action comprehension |
| Legibility | Can people distinguish the characters they need? | Recognition checks with the actual characters and font files under relevant conditions |
| Readability | Can people follow and read the content in its intended context? | Reading tasks, understanding, reported comfort, and observation |

These are project working distinctions. Font construction is only part of the outcome. Wording, layout, presentation, language, and user controls can matter. Select families first; evaluate later styling choices in the next stage. A reference specimen helps inspect a candidate but is not a complete reading test or an accessibility certification.

## Example assessment prompts

Use these only when they answer an unresolved question in the brief:

1. What do readers need to understand or do?
2. Will they mostly scan short labels, read paragraphs, or identify exact characters such as codes?
3. Which languages appear, and where?
4. Are there known needs affecting reading, character recognition, focus, or text access?
5. Where and under what conditions will people read?

Offer “not sure” and a place to add another need. Ask for functional needs rather than requiring private diagnoses. Collect only information that changes a design decision or a useful test. Ask about age or audience identity when it resolves a specific issue, then investigate the underlying need instead of inferring it.

A future explanation could say, “We prioritised distinguishable characters because readers need to check reference numbers.” That claim requires actual evidence of the candidate’s character behaviour. Do not publish “best for seniors,” “dyslexia-friendly,” or similar audience claims without adequate evidence and clear scope.
